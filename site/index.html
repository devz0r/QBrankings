How it works

The formula

CQR grades every snap a quarterback is responsible for, in two currencies: expected points (what the play did to the team's scoring chances given down, distance and field position) and adjusted yards (the box score, done right). It then strips out the things most clearly outside the quarterback's hands and puts the result on a 0-100 scale. What's left is opponent-adjusted quarterback production: pass protection, receivers and play-calling still show up in it, as they do in every public QB stat.

// 1. value every QB play against an average play of its kind
A  = ⅓ × (EPA* − avg) + ⅔ × (AY − avg) ÷ 9

EPA* = EPA
     + 4 × (lost − p)     on the QB's own fumbles
     × ½                  on pass interference drawn

AY   = pass yds + rush yds − sack yds
     + 20 × TD − 45 × INT − 45 × p per fumble
     (no INT charge on end-of-half heaves or
      3rd/4th-and-10+ throws of 20+ air yards)

// avg: league average for a dropback, or for an NFL run
//      in the same situation (short yardage or not)
// p:   how often that kind of fumble is lost league-wide

// 2. weight crunch time, adjust for defenses, add a cushion
              Σ w×A − Σ w×D
V  = ──────────────────────────────    w = 1.25 in 4th qtr / OT
     max(dropbacks, ¾ × plays) + 100        within 8, else 1
                                       D = defense faced

// 3. 0-100 scale, 50 = league average
CQR = 100 ÷ (1 + e^(−V ÷ 0.085))

  1. Count every play the QB owns

    Passes, sacks, scrambles, botched snaps, designed runs, sneaks, two-point tries and drawn pass interference. Kneel-downs and spikes are ignored, and so are plays wiped out by a teammate's penalty.

  2. Value it in points and in yards

    Expected points know that 4 yards on 3rd-and-3 beats 9 yards on 3rd-and-12. Adjusted yards know what the box score knows. Each catches the other's blind spots, so CQR blends them: one third points, two thirds yards, the mix that tested best on 2016-2020. Nine adjusted yards count as one point.

  3. Take out what clearly isn't the QB's doing

    Fumbles are charged at their average cost, because who falls on the ball is luck. Drawn pass interference counts half, teammates' penalties are ignored, and a deep interception on 3rd-and-long, which works like a punt, only costs what expected points say it cost. Protection, drops and play design can't be separated cleanly with public data, so they remain part of the rating.

  4. Compare each play with an average play of its kind

    Dropbacks are measured against the league's average dropback. Designed runs are measured against the league's average run in the same situation (short yardage or not), and they add to the QB's total without diluting the passing rate. That is how CQR counts dual threats. To keep it fair, the denominator never drops below three quarters of all the QB's plays, and players who mostly run (gadget QBs) aren't listed.

  5. Weight crunch time a little

    Plays in the 4th quarter or overtime of a one-score game count 1.25 times. Clutch play matters, but not so much that a handful of snaps decide a season.

  6. Adjust for the defenses faced

    Each defense is rated by what it allows to other teams' quarterbacks, regressed heavily toward average early in the year, and the QB is credited or charged for the defenses on the schedule.

  7. Make it earn the sample

    Every QB starts with 100 dropbacks of league-average play. One hot game can't vault a backup to No. 1. The cushion still matters: at 300 dropbacks it pulls a rating 25% of the way toward average, and about 14% by the end of a full season. Then the per-dropback value goes on a 0-100 curve where 50 is league average and 90-plus is an MVP-level season.

Questions

FAQ

Why is a QB with more yards ranked below one with fewer?

CQR is a rate. A quarterback who throws 45 times a game for 300 yards is less efficient than one who throws 28 times for 260, and the extra attempts are usually the team trailing, not extra quality. The "Points per game" columns show total value if you want volume in the picture: they add up what the QB produced compared with an average quarterback on the same number of snaps.

How does CQR treat sacks?

As the drive-killers they are. A sack costs the yards and usually the down, and that is close to two points of expected value. Research on pressure-to-sack rates shows quarterbacks own a large share of their sacks, so they are charged in full.

Why does a recovered fumble count against the QB?

Because putting the ball on the ground is the mistake. Whether a teammate or a defender falls on it is close to a coin flip, so CQR charges every fumble its average cost. That makes a fumble-prone QB look worse than the "fumbles lost" line suggests, and an unlucky one look a little better.

Why doesn't CQR count wins?

Wins belong to the whole roster. CQR measures the plays the quarterback is responsible for. Even so, it tracks winning more closely than passer rating, ANY/A or Total QBR do (see the track record).

What about garbage time?

It was tested. Discounting blowout snaps made rankings less stable and didn't fix a single eye-test problem, because on a per-play basis garbage time cuts both ways: soft prevent coverage, but also desperate throws into it. Crunch time gets the small 1.25 weight instead.

Why did a QB's rating move during a bye week?

The opponents kept playing. As the season goes on, the ratings of the defenses already faced get sharper, and the schedule adjustment moves with them.

Who counts as qualified?

At least 14 dropbacks per team game, the same bar the NFL uses for passer rating attempts. Part-time QBs are still rated; switch on "Include part-time QBs" to see them.

Where does the data come from, and when does it update?

Play-by-play from nflverse (nflfastR), which includes the expected-points model. The site rebuilds itself automatically on Monday, Tuesday and Friday mornings during the season, so Sunday, Monday and Thursday games are in by the time you read it.
